**What it is**

A tablet learning app for students that feels like a game, not homework. Students pick their grade and work through short "trails" of activities across Math, Reading/ELA, and Science, earning progress along the way with a mascot guide. The look and feel changes by age. Younger grades lean more playful and game like, high school leans more toward a clean, mature study tool feel, but both stay standards aligned underneath.

This will be a downloadable app for tablets and phones, and also a website. The app is the main experience for kids. The website is there for parents and teachers to log in from a browser, check progress, and manage accounts without installing anything.

Starting scope is grades K through 12, aligned to Texas TEKS standards, with every activity tagged to the skill it teaches and a parent/teacher view showing exactly what's covered.

Long term goal is all states, not just Texas. Content gets built around the underlying skills, not Texas specific codes, so it can map to other states' standards as we expand instead of rebuilding everything per state.

**How it makes money**

Parent subscription first. Free tier plus paid tier, same model as ABCmouse, Khan Academy Kids, and Prodigy. It's also the legally cleanest option since COPPA bars targeted ads to kids under 13, so ad supported isn't really viable here.

School and district licensing comes later, and it's the bigger opportunity. Texas has a formal review process (IMRA) for state approved instructional materials, which schools can then buy with state funding. Bigger payoff, slower process. Pursue this once the product is proven.

**Government funding: SBIR grants**

There's a federal grant program worth going after called SBIR (Small Business Innovation Research). It's a grant, not a loan and not equity, so no repayment and no giving up ownership. The Department of Education runs its own version through the Institute of Education Sciences, called ED/IES SBIR, built specifically for education technology products like this one. That's the best fit here.

How much it pays: Phase IA and Phase IB awards are each $250,000 for about 9 months. Phase IA is for a company with little or no working prototype yet, Phase IB is for adding onto a prototype that already exists. After a Phase IA or IB award, you can apply for Phase II, which pays $1,000,000 over 2 years. Total possible across both phases is $1.25 million. There's also a Direct to Phase II track at $1,000,000, but that one is meant for scaling something a university or nonprofit researcher already built, so it probably doesn't apply to us.

Past deadlines: the FY2025 round was due January 8, 2025. The FY2026 round was due June 29, 2026, later than usual because the whole federal SBIR program lapsed on September 30, 2025 and didn't get reauthorized until April 13, 2026. It's now authorized through September 30, 2031, so that risk is off the table for a while.

Upcoming: right now there's no open ED/IES SBIR solicitation, the FY2026 window already closed. It usually opens a new round every year, historically in fall or winter with a deadline 6 to 8 weeks later, but the exact FY2027 date hasn't been posted yet as of this writing. Check ies.ed.gov and sbir.gov directly for the announcement, and start prepping before it drops since SAM.gov and Grants.gov registration alone can take weeks.

Also worth knowing about: NSF (National Science Foundation) runs a broader SBIR program open to nearly any technology, not education specific, with bigger caps: Phase I up to $305,000, Phase II up to $1,250,000. They run multiple deadlines a year. The next one is November 4, 2026.

What it takes to apply for ED/IES specifically: a team of 3 to 5 people covering the educational, technical, and business sides, a real pilot study with an actual school or classroom for Phase I, letters from schools or partners saying they'd realistically use the product, and eventually a short video demo. Around 5 to 7 percent of Phase I proposals get funded, so this needs to be treated like a real proposal, not a form to fill out.

*Source: ies.ed.gov (ED/IES SBIR program pages), nsf.gov SBIR/STTR solicitation NSF 26-510, and sbir.gov. Figures current as of August 2026. Confirm current amounts and dates on the official sites before applying.*

**Legal setup: ownership, entity, and branding (Greg and Derrick, this is on us)**

None of this is built yet, and it needs an actual conversation plus probably a lawyer, but here's what needs deciding and roughly how each option works.

The 50/50 split. Splitting ownership evenly is simple to agree to, but it means neither person can outvote the other, so if we ever disagree on something big there's no built in way to break the tie. Worth deciding now, while things are friendly: how ties on real decisions get resolved, what happens if one person stops putting in time or money, whether the split is locked in right away or vests over time (common for co founders, so someone who leaves after a month doesn't keep half the company), and who owns the code, name, and design work already created before any of this is on paper.

Entity options to look into:

* DBA, also called an assumed name. This just registers a name for a business, filed with the county clerk. It does not create a legal entity and does not protect personal assets. Two people running a business together without forming a real entity are legally a general partnership by default, which means each of us could be personally on the hook for what the other does in the business's name. Cheapest and fastest option, but probably not the right long term setup for two owners.
* LLC, or limited liability company. This is the standard choice for something like this. It protects personal assets if the business gets sued or owes money, and it can be owned 50/50 by two members with the split and rules written into an operating agreement. In Texas, filing the Certificate of Formation with the Secretary of State costs $300. After that, Texas requires a Franchise Tax Report and a Public Information Report every year by May 15, filed with the Comptroller, not the Secretary of State. Revenue under about $2.47 million currently owes no actual tax, but the No Tax Due report still has to be filed on time to stay in good standing.
* Trust. A trust is normally a tool for holding and passing on assets, like for estate planning, not for actively running a two person operating business day to day. It's worth asking a lawyer directly whether a trust makes sense here for something specific, like holding the trademark or IP separately down the road, but for running the business itself, an LLC is almost certainly the right tool, not a trust.

Operating agreement. If we go the LLC route, the operating agreement is the actual document that spells out the 50/50 split and everything around it: ownership percentage, who contributed what (cash, time, existing code or ideas), each person's role and decision making authority, how profits and distributions get handled, what happens if one of us wants out or wants to sell their half, and a clear assignment clause making sure the app's code, name, and content legally belong to the LLC itself, not to whichever of us personally wrote or designed it. This is the single most important document to get right early, since it's a lot easier to agree on now than after there's real money or a real disagreement involved.

Branding. "Creative Learning Academy" is marked as a working title at the top of this document, so it's worth confirming the name is actually available (as a domain, on app stores, and as a trademark) before spending money to lock it in anywhere. Once a final name is picked, check that it's not already trademarked for software or education services through the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (downloadable apps and educational services fall under different trademark classes), check the domain and app store name are available, and consider registering the trademark once the name and business structure are both settled, not before.

None of this is legal advice, just a map of what to go figure out. An actual business or startup attorney, plenty of whom do a flat fee for LLC formation plus a co founder operating agreement, is worth the cost here given two people are splitting ownership.

**Compliance to build around from day one**

* COPPA applies specifically to kids under 13. That means verifiable parental consent, minimal data collection, documented retention and deletion, and no targeted ads. Accounts belong to the parent, not the child, for that age range.
* Older students, ages 13 to 17, aren't covered by COPPA, but they are covered by broader Texas minor protection laws like the App Store Accountability Act and the SCOPE Act. Different rules apply. Being past the COPPA age isn't a free pass.
* TEKS alignment is mapped by strand and topic for now, across all grade bands (elementary, middle, high school). It needs a real review from a teacher or curriculum specialist before we can claim certified alignment.

**Placement test prep courses**

Along with the core grade level curriculum, the app should offer placement test prep for every grade. These are short courses built around the tests families actually use to move a student between tracks: gifted and talented screening, magnet or charter school entrance exams, AP or dual credit qualifying exams, grade skipping or acceleration assessments, and district run math or reading placement tests that decide whether a student lands in honors, advanced, or remedial classes. This is a different bucket from the core STAAR aligned content. It's optional, higher stakes, and only matters to families actively trying to test into something.

**Note to self: school district guidelines are not all the same**

TEKS and STAAR are the state floor, but individual districts layer their own stuff on top, and it's not uniform. Different pacing guides, extra local benchmark tests, different qualifying scores for gifted and talented or advanced placement, different report card standards. Placement test content especially can't be treated as one size for the whole state.

Before actually building the placement prep courses:
* Look into how placement tests and cutoffs differ across a few big Texas districts first, like Houston ISD, Dallas ISD, Austin ISD, and Fort Worth ISD, to see how much they actually diverge from each other.
* Decide on a content model before building anything: one shared placement track with notes for each district, or fully separate tracks per district. This affects how the data gets structured from the start.
* Don't advertise or label any placement content as matching a specific district's test until it's actually been checked against that district's real guidelines.

**Roadmap**

Phase 1: Prototype. Working K through 12 mockup, mock data, no real accounts.
Phase 2: COPPA compliant account model. Parent versus child accounts, consent flow, minimal data.
Phase 3: Launch in Texas. Real accounts, real subscription billing, app and website both live.
Phase 4: Texas IMRA state adoption. Get approved for school and district purchasing.
Phase 5: Expand state by state. Map content to other states' standards.

**Build phases: iOS, Android, and website**

Trying to build all three platforms at the same time from day one isn't realistic. This is the order to actually build them in, and it runs alongside the roadmap above rather than replacing it.

Build phase 1: Core content and prototype. Get the actual lessons, activities, and grading logic working in one place first, before worrying about which platform it runs on. This is the same as Roadmap Phase 1.

Build phase 2: iOS app. Build and test the real app on iOS first.

Build phase 3: Android app. Port the same app and content over to Android once iOS is stable and working.

Build phase 4: Website. Build the parent and teacher facing website on top of the same backend and content used by the two apps, so everything stays in sync.

Build phase 5: All three live together. iOS app, Android app, and website all launched at once, all synced to the same accounts and progress data, with a polish pass across all three before wider release.

**Curriculum reference: STAAR study guide**

Before building Phase 1 content, `staar-guide/` holds a full breakdown of exactly what's tested on the STAAR test, Texas's current standardized test, sourced from the official TEKS standards behind it. It's organized grade by grade and subject by subject, with the tested grades and courses called out. It covers Reading/Language Arts, Math, Science, and Social Studies, plus the five high school end of course exams: Algebra I, English I, English II, Biology, and U.S. History. This is what Phase 1's content map should get built against, so activities line up with what students are actually accountable for.
